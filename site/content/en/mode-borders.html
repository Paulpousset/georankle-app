The land borders game

Two countries, one route to find. Each step must share a land border with the last — and the shortest path is almost never the one you picture.

Find the shortest route

Three lives, one globe, and two countries to connect.

How it works

The game gives you a starting country and a destination. Your job is to link them by typing intermediate countries, each of which must touch the previous one by a land border. The globe highlights your chain as you go: you watch the route take shape.

Three rules only:

  • each country you type must share a land border with the last one in the chain — straits and tunnels do not count;
  • a non-neighbouring country costs a life, and you have three per route;
  • fewer steps = more points.

What makes the mode addictive is that it reveals adjacencies nobody expects. China touches Afghanistan. Russia touches North Korea. Morocco and Mauritania are neighbours. And there are absolute dead ends: from Australia or from Madagascar, no land route exists at all.

The neighbour count is the game's own: France is metropolitan here (no border with Brazil or Suriname via French Guiana), and territories outside the {{countries}} countries — Kosovo, Western Sahara, Hong Kong — create no link.

Frequently asked

Which country has the most neighbours?
China and Russia, with fourteen land borders each. Brazil follows with nine, as do the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Germany.
Why does France have only eight neighbours?
Because the game uses metropolitan France. Counting French Guiana would add Brazil and Suriname. The same convention applies to Silhouette mode.
Can any two countries be linked?
No. Island countries — Madagascar, Iceland, Japan, nearly all of Oceania and the Caribbean — have no land border, so the game never offers them as a step.
Do maritime borders count?
No, only land borders. The Channel Tunnel, for instance, does not make the United Kingdom a neighbour of France in the game.

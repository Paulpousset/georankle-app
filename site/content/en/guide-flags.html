The flags of the world: families, symbols and lookalikes

There are not two hundred flags to learn by heart. There are a dozen families, a few dozen exceptions, and a handful of traps. Once the reading grid is in place, most of it sticks by itself.

Guide · 8 min read · updated August 2026

A national flag is never a decorative pattern picked at random. It is compressed history: an alliance, a revolution, a religion, an independence. Countries that share a past almost always share colours — and that is precisely what makes memorising them possible.

The Nordic crosses

Five Scandinavian countries carry the same construction: a cross shifted towards the hoist, its vertical arm closer to the mast than to the fly. This is the Scandinavian cross, born from the Danish Dannebrog — the oldest national flag still in use, traditionally dated to 1219.

  • Denmark: white cross on red.
  • Sweden: yellow cross on blue.
  • Norway: blue cross fimbriated white, on red.
  • Finland: blue cross on white.
  • Iceland: red cross fimbriated white, on blue — the exact chromatic inverse of Norway.

The Norway / Iceland pair is the only real difficulty in the family: same shapes, same three colours, permuted. Remember that Iceland is blue like its sea with volcanic red inside; Norway is red with blue inside.

The tricolours, or the descendants of 1789

The French flag set a pattern far beyond Europe. Three equal vertical bands: Italy, Ireland, Belgium, Romania, Chad, Mali, Guinea, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire and Mexico all reuse the structure. The horizontal tricolours — the Netherlands, Russia, Germany — form a second branch, older in the case of the Dutch Republic.

The lookalikes
  • Chad and Romania: blue, yellow, red, in the same order. The only difference is the shade of blue, darker on the Chadian side. It is the hardest pair in the entire world repertoire.
  • Ireland and Côte d'Ivoire: same colours, reversed order. Ireland starts with green at the hoist, Côte d'Ivoire with orange.
  • Monaco and Indonesia: red above, white below, identically. Only the proportions separate them — the Monegasque flag is stockier.
  • Poland: white above, red below. The exact inverse of the previous two.
  • The Netherlands and Luxembourg: red, white, blue. The Luxembourgish blue is noticeably lighter, and the flag longer.
  • Slovenia, Slovakia and Russia: the same white-blue-red Slavic tricolour. The first two carry a shield, placed higher and further left on Slovenia's.

The pan-African colours

Red, yellow and green are the colours of Ethiopia, the only African country never durably colonised. At the time of independence, in the 1950s and 1960s, many states adopted them as a sign of African continuity.

Ghana, Senegal, Mali, Guinea, Cameroon, Benin, Togo, Guinea-Bissau, Burkina Faso: the palette is the same, the arrangement changes. Ghana adds a black star, Senegal a green star, Cameroon a yellow one. Mali and Guinea are mirrors of each other: green-yellow-red for the first, red-yellow-green for the second.

The pan-Arab colours

Red, white, black and green form the other great family. They come from the flag of the 1916 Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire, each standing for a dynasty or an era of Arab history.

Egypt, Iraq, Syria, Yemen and Sudan share three horizontal red-white-black bands, told apart by a central emblem: the Eagle of Saladin for Egypt, the Allahu akbar inscription for Iraq, two green stars for Syria, nothing for Yemen. Jordan, Palestine and Sudan add a red or green triangle at the hoist. Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates and Libya rearrange the same four colours differently.

Crescents, stars and religious symbols

A crescent with a star most often signals a Muslim identity: Turkey, Tunisia, Algeria, Pakistan, Mauritania, Azerbaijan, Comoros, Malaysia. The Maldives carry a crescent alone, with no star.

Conversely the Christian cross structures the Swiss, Greek, Dominican and Tongan flags, alongside the Nordic crosses above. Greece combines both registers: nine stripes for the nine syllables of the independence cry, and a cross in the canton.

The one-of-a-kind flags

  • Nepal: the only national flag that is not rectangular. Two stacked pennants carrying a sun and a moon.
  • Switzerland and the Vatican: the only two square flags. At the Olympics the Swiss flag is actually flown in a rectangular version, by exception to national protocol.
  • Belize: the only flag depicting human beings — two woodcutters, one mestizo, one black, framing a mahogany tree.
  • Mozambique: the only one showing a modern automatic weapon, an AK-47 crossed with a hoe, a legacy of the independence struggle.
  • Cambodia: the only one depicting a monument, the temple of Angkor Wat.
  • Cyprus and Kosovo: the only two showing a map of their own territory. In both cases it is a deliberate de-escalation — avoiding the symbols of the communities present.
  • Bhutan: a dragon holding jewels, on a yellow and orange diagonal.
  • Palau: a yellow disc offset towards the hoist, on blue. It is not a sun but a full moon.

The disc trap

Three flags play on the same solid-circle motif, and are readily confused in a fast quiz:

  • Japan: red disc perfectly centred on white.
  • Bangladesh: red disc slightly offset towards the hoist, on green. The offset is deliberate: it compensates for the optical effect of a flag in the wind.
  • Palau: yellow disc offset, on light blue.

The Union Jacks of the Pacific

Four independent states keep the British flag in the upper canton: Australia, New Zealand, Fiji and Tuvalu. The first two are told apart by their stars — Australia adds a large Commonwealth Star below the canton and six stars in total, while New Zealand shows only the four red, white-fimbriated stars of the Southern Cross.

It makes for an excellent quiz reflex: more stars, and white ones → Australia; four red stars → New Zealand.

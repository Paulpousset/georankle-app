The capitals of Europe

Forty-five countries, forty-five capitals — and a handful of them that catch out nine players in ten. Here is the full list, and what to know before reciting it.

Reference · {{count:europe}} countries · game data, updated August 2026

Europe is the continent where capitals are oldest and most stable: Rome, Athens, Lisbon and Paris have held the role for centuries, sometimes millennia. Good news for memory — nothing has moved recently — but it also makes the exceptions all the more treacherous.

The five classic traps

Switzerland is trap number one. Not Zurich, the largest city, nor Geneva, the most international: the capital is Bern, chosen in 1848 precisely because it dominated nobody. Switzerland does not even call it a capital, but a federal city.

The Netherlands comes next. The constitution names Amsterdam as the capital, but the government, parliament, Supreme Court and the king all sit in The Hague. Both answers are defensible in conversation; in a quiz, it is Amsterdam.

Turkey does not appear on this list: in the split used here it is Asian. Its capital is still Ankara, not Istanbul — the same confusion applies to Kazakhstan, whose western part is European but which is filed under Asia.

Russia, by contrast, is counted here as European: that is where most of its population and its capital, Moscow, are.

Finally the micro-states — Vaduz for Liechtenstein, San Marino for San Marino, Andorra la Vella for Andorra, Monaco for Monaco — are the ones nobody revises and the ones that come up most. Four names to learn, and a whole category of questions is settled.

Frequently asked

What is the capital of Switzerland?
Bern. Neither Zurich nor Geneva: Bern was designated in 1848 as the “federal city” because it overshadowed no canton.
Amsterdam or The Hague?
Amsterdam is the constitutional capital of the Netherlands; The Hague is the seat of government, parliament and the monarchy. In a quiz, the expected answer is Amsterdam.

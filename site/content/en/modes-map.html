World map games: globe, borders and comparisons

Knowing a country exists is not enough: you also need to place it, know its neighbours and have a sense of its size. These three modes train the map itself.

Multiple-choice quizzes test what you know about a country. Map games test what you know about countries relative to each other: where they are, which ones touch, which is bigger or more populous. It is the part of geography that sticks best once learned, because it rests on a mental picture rather than a list.

The 3D globe starts with raw location: a name, a globe to spin, a point to drop. The borders game asks you to link two countries by the shortest land route, which means knowing exact neighbours and not just the region. Higher or Lower works on orders of magnitude: population, area and other rankings, two countries at a time.

The three modes complement each other. A player who places countries well on the globe but doesn’t know their neighbours will improve fast at borders; one who knows the map but misjudges sizes will find in Higher or Lower a way to correct those instincts.

Place countries on a map: the 3D globe

A country name appears. You have to find it on a real globe you spin with your finger — not on a flat map where Africa looks smaller than Greenland.

A country name appears, you rotate the globe and tap the right territory. One finger to spin, two to zoom; on a desktop, the mouse and wheel do the same.

Choosing a sphere over a map is not decorative. Every flat projection distorts: the most common one, Mercator, inflates high latitudes so much that Greenland looks as large as Africa, when it is fourteen times smaller. A globe does not lie about area, and it teaches real relative positions along the way — that Norway sits at the latitude of Alaska, that Sicily is further south than Tunis.

Micro-states are handled: Monaco, San Marino, the Vatican, Nauru, Tuvalu and a few others are too small to tap at globe scale, so they appear as a tappable dot as soon as you zoom in far enough.

The score combines accuracy and time. As everywhere in the game, a solo run can be limited to one continent.

The land borders game

Two countries, one route to find. Each step must share a land border with the last — and the shortest path is almost never the one you picture.

The game gives you a starting country and a destination. Your job is to link them by typing intermediate countries, each of which must touch the previous one by a land border. The globe highlights your chain as you go: you watch the route take shape.

Three rules only:

  • each country you type must share a land border with the last one in the chain — straits and tunnels do not count;
  • a non-neighbouring country costs a life, and you have three per route;
  • fewer steps = more points.

What makes the mode addictive is that it reveals adjacencies nobody expects. China touches Afghanistan. Russia touches North Korea. Morocco and Mauritania are neighbours. And there are absolute dead ends: from Australia or from Madagascar, no land route exists at all.

The neighbour count is the game's own: France is metropolitan here (no border with Brazil or Suriname via French Guiana), and territories outside the {{countries}} countries — Kosovo, Western Sahara, Hong Kong — create no link.

Higher or Lower: which of these two countries is above?

Two countries, one criterion, one question: which is ahead? The winner stays on, a new country steps in, and the chain runs until your first mistake.

Two countries appear with a criterion — population, area, tourist arrivals, GDP — and you pick the one with the higher value.

The winning country stays on screen, a new challenger arrives, and the chain continues. A single mistake ends the run: the whole point is to go as far as you can.

The difficulty rises by itself, because the game gradually narrows the gaps. Comparing China and Luxembourg is trivial; comparing Kenya and Sudan on population is much less so. And the criterion changes on every question: a country that just won on area can lose the next round on tourism.

It is the mode that installs orders of magnitude best. After a few runs you instinctively know that Nigeria outweighs Germany on population, that Algeria is larger than Greenland, or that France receives more tourists than the United States.

Frequently asked

Why a globe rather than a map?
Because every flat map distorts. The Mercator projection makes Greenland look as big as Africa when it is fourteen times smaller. A globe gives real areas and real relative positions.
Can you tap the tiny countries?
Yes. Monaco, San Marino, the Vatican, Nauru, Tuvalu and the other micro-states appear as tappable dots once you zoom in close enough.
Does the globe work in a browser?
Yes, with no install and no plugin. It also works offline in the mobile app.
Can I practise one continent?
Yes: solo play lets you restrict the run to Europe, Africa, Asia, North America, South America or Oceania.
Which country has the most neighbours?
China and Russia, with fourteen land borders each. Brazil follows with nine, as do the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Germany.
Why does France have only eight neighbours?
Because the game uses metropolitan France. Counting French Guiana would add Brazil and Suriname. The same convention applies to Silhouette mode.
Can any two countries be linked?
No. Island countries — Madagascar, Iceland, Japan, nearly all of Oceania and the Caribbean — have no land border, so the game never offers them as a step.
Do maritime borders count?
No, only land borders. The Channel Tunnel, for instance, does not make the United Kingdom a neighbour of France in the game.
Does the criterion change every question?
Yes. A country that just won on population can lose the next round on area or tourism. That is what stops you playing on autopilot.
How many mistakes can you make?
One: the run ends on the first error. The score is the length of the chain.
Which criteria are used?
The same {{themes}} as Rankle: population, area, GDP, tourism, coastline length, UNESCO sites, and about thirty other public indicators.

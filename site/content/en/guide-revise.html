How to study geography: a 20-minutes-a-day method

Learning “all the countries in the world” is a discouraging goal. Cut up properly — an order, a medium, a rhythm — it is a matter of a few weeks of short sessions.

Guide · 9 min read · updated August 2026

World geography has a rare property: it is finite. One hundred and ninety-five countries, as many capitals, a map that almost never changes. Unlike a language, there is a finish line. You just have to start in the right order.

Step 1 — The skeleton before the details

Never start with countries. Start with the landmarks that structure the map, the ones everything else will hang on:

  • The oceans and major seas: Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Black Sea, Baltic, South China Sea, Caribbean.
  • The ranges: Alps, Himalaya, Andes, Rockies, Urals, Atlas.
  • The great rivers: Nile, Amazon, Congo, Mississippi, Danube, Ganges, Mekong, Yangtze.
  • The deserts: Sahara, Gobi, Kalahari, Atacama, Arabian Desert.
  • The lines: equator, tropics, polar circles.

These landmarks act as geographic anchors. Knowing that Mali is “south of the Sahara, on the Niger” beats knowing it is “somewhere in West Africa”: the first lets you find it on a blank map, the second does not.

Step 2 — Countries, from the best known to the rarest

The learning order matters enormously, and alphabetical order is the worst of all. Learn by decreasing familiarity: first the thirty or forty countries everyone names spontaneously, then the next tier, down to the micro-states and Pacific islands.

Two reasons. First, well-known countries are the ones that come up in the news and in conversation: every review is reinforced for free by daily life. Second, they are large: they occupy the map, and serve as landmarks for placing the small ones. You learn Cambodia as “between Thailand and Vietnam”, not the other way round.

Suggested order
  1. Western Europe and North America — largely known already.
  2. The major powers and large countries of Asia and South America.
  3. Central and Eastern Europe and the Balkans — dense, lots of borders.
  4. North and West Africa, then East and Southern Africa.
  5. Central Asia, the Caucasus, the Gulf.
  6. The Caribbean, Oceania, the micro-states. The last third, and the most expensive.

Step 3 — Learn to place, not just to name

Knowing a country's name and being able to place it on a map are two distinct skills, and the second does not follow from the first. Three complementary approaches:

Neighbourhood. Always learn a country with its neighbours. “Burkina Faso is surrounded by Mali, Niger, Benin, Togo, Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire” anchors seven countries at once. That is the principle of the Borders mode: linking two countries step by step forces you to build a connected mental map rather than a list.

Shape. Some outlines are unforgettable once noticed: the Italian boot, Chile as a ribbon, India as a triangle, Norway stretched thin, The Gambia as a finger along its river, Lesotho as a hole inside South Africa. Working on outlines alone — no name, no colour — forces memory to encode the geometry.

The globe. Flat maps lie: the Mercator projection inflates high latitudes enormously. Greenland looks as big as Africa there, when it would fit fourteen times inside it. Africa is larger than China, the United States, India and much of Europe combined. Revising at least part of the time on a globe corrects those distortions.

Step 4 — Deal with the systematic confusions

A handful of pairs generate most errors. They are not fixed by revising more, but by working on them together and explicitly:

ConfusionHow to separate them
Austria / AustraliaAustria is alpine and landlocked; Australia is a continent. The near-identical English names catch out native speakers too.
Slovenia / SlovakiaSlovenia touches the Adriatic and Italy; Slovakia is landlocked, south of Poland.
Niger / NigeriaNiger is landlocked, Sahelian and larger; Nigeria is coastal, on the Gulf of Guinea, and by far Africa's most populous country.
The two CongosThe Democratic Republic of the Congo has Kinshasa as its capital; the Republic of the Congo has Brazzaville. The two cities face each other across the river.
The GuineasGuinea (Conakry), Guinea-Bissau, Equatorial Guinea — the last much further east, on the Gulf of Guinea. And Papua New Guinea, on the other side of the world.
Dominica / Dominican RepublicDominica is a small island in the Lesser Antilles; the Dominican Republic shares Hispaniola with Haiti.
Suriname / GuyanaWest to east along the coast: Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana.
Slavonia / SloveniaSlavonia is not a country: it is a region of eastern Croatia. Recognising false leads is part of the exercise.

Step 5 — The rhythm

Twenty minutes a day, split up:

  • 5 minutes reviewing what you got wrong yesterday. It is the most profitable part of the session, and the one people skip most often.
  • 10 minutes of new material: one region, about ten countries, no more.
  • 5 minutes of mixed practice: random questions on everything seen so far. That is what stops knowledge from staying compartmentalised by continent.

Regularity before duration: a daily twenty-minute session beats two hours at the weekend by a wide margin. Memory is built in the gaps between sessions, not during them.

The plateau trap

After two or three weeks, progress slows abruptly. It is mechanical: all that is left are the hard countries, the ones with no anchor in your general knowledge. At that point change angle rather than push harder — switch from flags to outlines, or from capitals to position on the globe. The same country, approached differently, becomes learnable again.

Should you learn the numbers?

Populations, areas, GDP: there is no point memorising them precisely. What is useful are the orders of magnitude and the rankings — knowing that Indonesia is the fourth most populous country, that Canada is second by area, that Nigeria far outstrips Egypt in population.

That kind of knowledge builds very well by comparison: faced with “which is bigger?”, the brain learns the ranking without ever memorising a single number. That is the principle behind ranking and comparison modes, and it is infinitely more durable than a table of figures.

How long to learn it all?

For an adult starting from average general knowledge, at twenty minutes a day: roughly two weeks for the flags of the major countries, four to six weeks for all {{countries}} flags and capitals, two to three months to add reliable placement on a blank map.

After that, upkeep costs very little: a few minutes a day is enough to hold what you have, provided you do not stop entirely for months.

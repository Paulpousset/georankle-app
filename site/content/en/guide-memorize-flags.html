How to memorise the flags of the world: a 4-week plan

Nobody learns {{countries}} flags by watching them scroll past. You learn them by grouping them, testing yourself, and spacing your reviews. Here is a concrete plan, at fifteen minutes a day.

Guide · 7 min read · updated August 2026

The difficulty is not the number. You already know hundreds of brand logos, faces and album covers by heart. The difficulty is that flags look alike: a few colours, a few geometric shapes, and a mass of neighbouring combinations. Raw visual memory saturates fast.

Three principles, all backed by learning research, solve most of the problem.

Principle 1 — Learn families, not units

Remembering twenty isolated flags is hard. Remembering “the Nordic cross family, five members, each with its own colour combination” is easy, because you are no longer memorising twenty images but one rule and five variants.

So always start with the structure:

  • The Nordic crosses: Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, Iceland.
  • The pan-African colours (red, yellow, green): Ghana, Senegal, Mali, Guinea, Cameroon, Benin, Togo…
  • The pan-Arab colours (red, white, black, green): Egypt, Iraq, Syria, Yemen, Jordan, Sudan, Kuwait, the UAE.
  • The vertical tricolours inherited from the French flag.
  • The Pacific Union Jacks: Australia, New Zealand, Fiji, Tuvalu.
  • The crescents: Turkey, Tunisia, Algeria, Pakistan, Mauritania, Azerbaijan, Comoros, Malaysia, the Maldives.

Each family is detailed in our guide The flags of the world: families, symbols and lookalikes. Once those six groups are in place, you have covered nearly a third of the world with six rules.

Principle 2 — Test yourself, don't re-read

This is the most common mistake: looking at a chart of flags and thinking “yes, I know that one”. That feeling of familiarity is not memory. It vanishes the moment the answer is no longer in front of you.

What builds durable memory is active recall: see the flag, search for the answer, sometimes get it wrong, then check. The retrieval effort is precisely what consolidates the trace — what research calls the testing effect. A ten-question quiz beats half an hour of staring at a poster.

Practical rule

If you did not get a single answer wrong during a session, it was too easy. The sweet spot sits around 70 to 80 % correct: enough success to stay motivated, enough errors to actually learn.

Principle 3 — Space it out rather than cram

Four fifteen-minute sessions spread over four days produce a markedly more durable memory than one hour-long session. The mechanism is well known: each time a memory is reactivated just as it starts to fade, it is reconsolidated more strongly.

Hence the value of a short daily appointment rather than a Sunday marathon. That is exactly the logic of a daily challenge: a handful of questions, every day, on different countries.

The mnemonics that actually work

A good mnemonic links the flag to something you already know. A few that hold up:

  • Japan: “the land of the rising sun” — centred red disc. Bangladesh has the same disc, offset, on the green of the rice fields.
  • Lebanon: a cedar, the national tree, cited in the Bible.
  • Canada: the maple leaf, the one you get syrup from.
  • Cyprus: a map of the island, in copper — Cyprus gave its name to copper, of which the island was the main producer in antiquity.
  • Bhutan: literally “the land of the thunder dragon”, with a dragon on the flag.
  • Mozambique: the only flag with an AK-47 — impossible to confuse once seen.
  • Nepal: the only non-rectangular one, two pennants like two Himalayan peaks.

For lookalike pairs, build a contrast rather than an association. Ireland / Côte d'Ivoire: Ireland is green, and the green is on the mast side, like the shamrock you pin near your heart. It does not matter that it is slightly arbitrary — a personal mnemonic always beats an elegant one.

The four-week plan

Week 1 — The families and Europe

Fifteen minutes a day. Learn the six families above, then Europe, the continent with the densest flag coverage and the one the families cover best. By the end of the week you should be able to name the five Nordic crosses without hesitating.

Week 2 — Africa and the Americas

Africa is where most points are lost, and paradoxically the most systematic continent: the pan-African palette covers a majority of cases. The Americas are easier — lots of star flags and horizontal stripes.

Week 3 — Asia, Oceania and the trap pairs

Add Asia and Oceania, then devote two sessions explicitly to the pairs you confuse: Chad / Romania, Monaco / Indonesia, Slovenia / Slovakia, Australia / New Zealand, Norway / Iceland. Work them side by side, never in isolation: it is the difference you need to encode, not the flag.

Week 4 — Everything mixed

Stop revising by continent. Mix everything, in random order: this is called interleaving, and it is the difference between “I recognise them when I know it's Africa” and “I recognise them”. This week will feel harder than week three. That is normal, and it is the sign that it is working.

The mistake to avoid

Do not endlessly re-learn the flags you already know. Useful time is time spent on the 30 or 40 that resist — usually the Pacific islands, the Caribbean and Central Africa. A good training tool should show you those more often.

And after that?

Once the flags are in, the most effective step is to attach them to something else: the country's position on the map, its capital, its shape. Each extra link is one more access route to the same memory — and that is what turns a quiz performance into real knowledge.
